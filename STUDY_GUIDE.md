# J1 study guide: everything you need to explain and defend the project

All numbers below come from `results/summary.json` (generated by `python -m analysis.run_all`).

---

## 1. The project in one paragraph

Compound is governed by COMP token votes. People usually judge whether a DAO is decentralised by looking at **token shares**: who holds how many votes. But holding 13% of the votes is not the same as having 13% of the *say*. Power depends on how often you can actually decide an outcome. We rebuilt every Compound GovernorBravo proposal and vote from raw Ethereum data and worked out each delegate's voting weight at each proposal. We then computed two standard **power indices**, Banzhaf and Shapley-Shubik, with our own dynamic-programming code, and compared them with token share and with what actually happened in each vote.

**Headline:** Compound looks like thousands of voters, but about 5 addresses could have decided almost every vote, and the largest delegate has more power than its token share suggests.

## 2. Background you need

### Ethereum basics
- **Block:** the chain advances in blocks (about 12 s each). Any state, such as balances, can be asked "as of block b".
- **Smart contract events (logs):** contracts emit events such as `Transfer`, `VoteCast` and `DelegateChanged`. We read them with the JSON-RPC call `eth_getLogs`.
- **Archive node:** a node that can answer questions about old blocks. We used free public archive endpoints, so no API key was needed.

### How Compound governance works
- **COMP token:** 10M total supply. One token is one vote, *but only if delegated*.
- **Delegation:** a holder must call `delegate(addr)`, either to themselves or to someone else, before their tokens count. All of a holder's tokens go to **one** delegate. It is **one-hop**: if A→B and B→C, A's tokens count for **B**, not C. Delegating to `0x0` undoes it, and tokens that are not delegated count for nobody.
- **A proposal's life:** created → voting opens at `startBlock` → closes at `endBlock` → it passes if **for > against and for ≥ 400,000 COMP (quorum)** → queued in a timelock → executed. It can also be canceled.
- **Vote weight:** fixed at `startBlock` (`getPriorVotes(voter, startBlock)`), so buying tokens mid-vote doesn't help. Vote options are for, against and abstain; abstain counts toward neither the result nor quorum.
- **Governor contracts:** Governor Alpha handled proposals 1–42. **GovernorBravo** handled proposals 43–393, from 2021 to January 2025, and is what we studied. Compound later moved to a new governor.

### Weighted voting games
- Players have weights w₁…wₙ and there is a **quota q**. A coalition S **wins** if its total weight w(S) ≥ q. This is written [q; w₁,…,wₙ].
- Player i is a **swing** for coalition S if S loses without i but wins with i: q − wᵢ ≤ w(S) < q.
- **Dummy (null) player:** never a swing, so its power is 0, even if its weight is above 0.

### The two power indices
| | Banzhaf | Shapley-Shubik |
|---|---|---|
| Idea | Over all coalitions of the *other* players (each equally likely), how often is i the swing? | Players join one at a time in a random order. How often is i the one who first pushes the total to the quota (the **pivot**)? |
| Formula | ψᵢ = #swings / 2ⁿ⁻¹; normalised βᵢ = ψᵢ / Σψ | φᵢ = Σ over swing coalitions S of \|S\|!(n−1−\|S\|)!/n! |
| Sums to 1? | Only after normalising | Always (if anyone can win) |

**Worked example: [51; 50, 30, 20].** The winning coalitions are {1,2}, {1,3} and {1,2,3}.
- Swings: player 1 has 3 ({2}, {3}, {2,3}), players 2 and 3 have 1 each. So Banzhaf = (3,1,1)/5 = **(0.6, 0.2, 0.2)**.
- Pivots in the 6 orderings: player 1 in 4, players 2 and 3 in 1 each. So Shapley-Shubik = **(2/3, 1/6, 1/6)**.
- Token shares are (0.5, 0.3, 0.2). **Power is not proportional to weight.**
- ⚠ The brief said Banzhaf = (0.5, 0.25, 0.25). That's wrong; we corrected it and noted it in the README.

### Other measures we compare against
- **Gini coefficient:** inequality of weights. 0 means everyone is equal, 1 means one holder has everything.
- **Nakamoto coefficient:** the fewest players who together hold more than 50%.
- **Potential power** (Fritsch et al.): could this voter have flipped *this actual vote* by switching sides? **Exercised power:** did removing its vote flip it?
- **Total-variation (TV) distance:** ½ Σ|xᵢ − yᵢ|, how far apart two distributions of power are (0 means identical).

## 3. What we did, step by step

### Step 1: Fetch raw data (`data/fetch.py`, `data/rpc.py`, `data/abi.py`)
- Pulled 8 event types with `eth_getLogs`:
  - **Governor:** ProposalCreated, VoteCast, ProposalCanceled/Queued/Executed.
  - **COMP token:** Transfer (**2.33M** events), DelegateChanged (16,038), DelegateVotesChanged.
- Block window: COMP deployment (9.6M) to 22.0M, fixed in `config.yaml`.
- Large block ranges are split automatically when an endpoint refuses them. Everything is cached in `data/raw/` (175 MB), so reruns work offline.
- We also read `state(id)`, `proposals(id)` and `quorumVotes()` on-chain, for cross-checks.
- We used `requests` plus our own ABI decoding instead of web3.

### Step 2: Process (`data/process.py`, `power/delegation.py`)
- Decoded proposals (351) and votes (14,396).
- **Snapshot replay:** went through all transfers and delegation events once, in block order, stopping at each proposal's `startBlock` (events *in* that block are included, as the protocol does). At each stop we know every balance and every delegation.
- **Voting weight** of delegate d = sum of the balances of everyone currently delegating to d (one-hop).
- **Players** = every address with weight > 0 (median **4,912** per proposal). Delegators are *not* separate players, because their tokens are already inside their delegate's weight.
- Also implemented **transitive** delegation (follow A→B→C to the end) as a sensitivity check. **Cycle rule:** addresses on a cycle keep their own tokens, and a chain that runs into a cycle stops at the first cycle address it meets.

### Step 3: Validate against the chain (`data/processed/validation.csv`)
| Check | Mismatches |
|---|---|
| Our weight vs the on-chain checkpoint, for every delegate at all 351 snapshots | **0** |
| Our weight vs each of the 14,396 actual vote weights | **0** |
| Our tallies vs `proposals(id)` | 0 (max difference 2e-10 COMP) |
| Our outcome vs `state(id)`: 281 executed, 33 defeated, 37 canceled | **0** |

This proves the delegation resolver and the snapshot rule are right.

### Step 4: Build each proposal's game (`power/game.py`)
- **Quota:** passing needs for > against and for ≥ Q. If every player votes for or against, that becomes **q = max(⌊W/2⌋+1, Q)**, where W is total weight.
  - Delegated weight is 1.76M–3.13M COMP, so the majority term (more than 1.5M) always beats the 400k quorum. **Quorum never binds in this game.**
- **Integer weights:** the DP needs whole numbers, so weights are rounded to units of u = W/20000 (about 156 COMP).
  - Players below u/2 round to 0 and become dummies: about **76** non-dummy players remain per proposal, and the dummies hold at most 0.12% of the weight.
  - Raising the resolution to 50000 changes any index by at most **0.00016**.

### Step 5: Compute the indices (`power/banzhaf.py`, `power/shapley.py`)
- **Brute force is impossible:** 2⁴⁹⁰⁰ coalitions or 4900! orderings.
- **Banzhaf DP:** p[x] = probability that a random coalition has weight x. Adding a player of weight v: p'[x] = ½(p[x] + p[x−v]). Player i's raw Banzhaf = P(q−wᵢ ≤ w(S) < q).
- **Shapley-Shubik DP:** P[k,x] = probability that a random *k-player* coalition has weight x. Adding a player: P'[k,x] = ((m+1−k)P[k,x] + k·P[k−1,x−v])/(m+1). Then φᵢ = (1/n) Σₖ Pₖ(q−wᵢ ≤ w(S) < q).
- **Tricks:**
  - Probabilities instead of counts, so nothing overflows (counts would reach C(n, n/2)).
  - Only weights below q are tracked.
  - Equal weights get equal power, so each distinct weight is computed once.
  - **Divide-and-conquer leave-one-out:** to get "everyone except i" we only ever *add* players. Removing a player is numerically unstable.
- **Speed:** about 1.5 s per proposal.
- **Tests** (`tests/`, **280** passing in about 2 s):
  - The known example, dummy players, symmetry, and Shapley-Shubik summing to 1.
  - DP equals brute force on random games with n ≤ 12, and equals the permutation definition for n ≤ 7.
  - Delegation: chains, cycles, self-delegation, undelegation, same-block ordering, and snapshots not affected by later events.
  - Flip sets match brute force.

### Step 6: Analyse (`analysis/run_all.py`, `analysis/beyond.py`, results in `results/`)
1. **Gap analysis:** token share vs Banzhaf vs Shapley-Shubik, per proposal and per address averaged over all proposals.
2. **Votes to outcomes:** a **flip set** is a group of players who, by changing only their own votes, would reverse the result (quorum applied).
   - For a passed proposal: for-voters switch to against, and non-voters join against.
   - For a defeated one: against-voters switch to for, and non-voters join for.
   - We find the **smallest** flip set, and check whether the top-k delegates alone form one.
3. **Participation game (extension):** players are only the actual for/against voters, weighted by votes cast, with the same quota rule. Quorum now matters.
4. **Prior work recomputed on our data:** Fritsch et al.'s Nakamoto coefficient, Gini, and potential/exercised power; Messias et al.'s voters needed to reach 50%.
5. **Robustness:** rounding resolution and transitive delegation.
6. **Outputs:** figures, CSV tables, `summary.json`, and `numbers.tex`, so every number on the slides is generated by code.

## 5. Likely questions and answers

**Why not just use token share or Gini?**
Share ignores how the other votes are spread out and what the quota is. In the example, 50% of the weight gave 60–67% of the power. The indices measure the ability to change outcomes, which is the definition of power.

**Banzhaf vs Shapley-Shubik: what's the difference?**
Banzhaf treats every coalition as equally likely and counts swings. Shapley-Shubik treats every *ordering* as equally likely and counts pivots, so it weights coalitions by size. They answer slightly different "what is power" models. We report both.

**Why is their rank correlation exactly 1?**
In a weighted game, a heavier player can do everything a lighter one can, so both indices are monotone in weight. They always rank players the same way. The real difference is in magnitude, which we measure with TV distance (median 0.015). Banzhaf gives the top delegate a little more power.

**Why does the biggest delegate get more power than its share?**
To reach a 50% quota it needs only a few partners, so it is a swing in a huge number of coalitions. Small holders rarely make or break a coalition. Concentration at the top is amplified, and everyone else is slightly compressed.

**Who are the players, and why not the token holders?**
Every address with voting weight above 0 at the snapshot. A delegator's tokens are already inside its delegate's weight, and only the delegate votes, so counting delegators too would double-count.

**Why one-hop delegation? The brief mentions chains and cycles.**
COMP itself is one-hop, and our weights match the chain exactly under that rule. We also implemented and tested transitive resolution with an explicit cycle rule. Switching to it moves at most 5.3% of weight in any proposal.

**Why the snapshot at `startBlock`?**
That is exactly what the protocol uses for vote weight (`getPriorVotes`). It's also why we can verify our weights against every single vote.

**How do you know your data is correct?**
0 mismatches against on-chain checkpoints, vote weights, tallies and states (Step 3).

**Why round the weights? Doesn't that change the answer?**
The DP runs over whole-number weights. The rounding error is at most 0.00016 on any index, and the dummies created hold at most 0.12% of the weight.

**Why not brute force? What's the complexity?**
There are 2⁴⁹⁰⁰ subsets, so enumeration is impossible. The DP costs O(n·q) per added player for Banzhaf and O(n²·q) for Shapley-Shubik, about 1.5 s per proposal with around 76 live players. Brute force is used only in tests.

**Why doesn't quorum matter in the main game?**
The main game assumes everyone votes, and then 50% of 1.76M–3.13M COMP is more than 400k. Real turnout is about 25%, so we added the participation game, where quorum binds in 259 of 314 proposals.

**What exactly is a flip set? Why include non-voters?**
A set of addresses that could reverse the result by changing only their own votes. Non-voting delegates held weight and *could* have voted, so they had power. That's why 28 of 33 defeats, mostly quorum failures, were reversible by a single large non-voter.

**Isn't "all voters participate and aren't strategic" unrealistic?**
Yes. It's the standard a-priori power assumption. We relax the all-vote part with the participation game. Strategic behaviour and coordination are out of scope and listed as limitations.

**One person can control many addresses. Doesn't that break this?**
It would make concentration *higher*, so our results are a lower bound. Fritsch et al. cluster addresses but admit it's "not bullet-proof". We don't cluster.

**What's new compared to Fritsch et al. and Messias et al.?**
Neither computes Banzhaf or Shapley-Shubik. Fritsch et al. use Gini, Nakamoto and potential power, which checks a swing at only the one realised vote. Messias et al. measure concentration and voting costs. We compute both full indices exactly for every proposal, and we reproduce their numbers (8 vs 8, 0.987 vs 0.986).

**Why is your 3.55 different from Messias et al.'s 3.18?**
Different scope: they include Governor Alpha and data to August 2024, while we cover Bravo proposals 43–393.

**Why not use The Graph like Fritsch et al.?**
The brief ranks an archive node first, and raw logs let us verify everything exactly. We still compare at their block 14.3M.

**What's the potential-power sanity check?**
If one voter can flip a vote by switching, then {that voter} is a flip set of size 1. The code asserts that every such proposal has a minimal flip set of size 1, and it holds.

**Canceled proposals?**
37 of them. They are kept in the dataset and the index tables, and excluded only from the votes-to-outcome analysis because there was no decision.

**What did you write yourselves vs use libraries for?**
We wrote all governance logic: delegation, snapshots, games, both indices, flip sets, Gini, Nakamoto and Spearman. Libraries are only for HTTP, the keccak hash, arrays, tables, plots, config and tests (`DEPENDENCIES.md`). No power-index library is used.

**Why does the top delegate's power jump around proposals 165, 225 and 290?**
Delegation changed, so the largest delegate's weight changed. We report addresses, not identities.

## 6. Limitations (say these before they're asked)
1. Addresses are not entities; concentration is probably understated.
2. A-priori indices assume non-strategic voters (partly relaxed by the participation game).
3. One DAO and one governor (Bravo, 2021–2025).
4. Integer rounding (effect at most 0.00016).
5. Snapshot at `startBlock` only (it matches the protocol).
